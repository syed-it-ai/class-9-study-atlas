Matter in Our Surroundings

Everything in this universe is made up of material which scientists have named ‘matter’. The air we breathe, food we eat, stones, clouds, stars, plants and animals, even a small drop of water or a particle of sand — everything is matter. We can also see as we look around that all the things mentioned above occupy space and have mass. In other words, they have both mass and volume.

Since early times, human beings have been trying to understand their surroundings. Early Indian philosophers classified matter in the form of five basic elements — the ‘Panch Tatva’ — air, earth, fire, sky and water. According to them everything, living or non-living, was made up of these five basic elements. Ancient Greek philosophers had arrived at a similar classification of matter.

Modern-day scientists have evolved two types of classification of matter based on their physical properties and chemical nature.

In this chapter we shall learn about matter based on its physical properties. Chemical aspects of matter will be taken up in subsequent chapters.

The SI unit of mass is kilogram (kg). The SI unit of volume is cubic metre (m³). The common unit of measuring volume is litre (L) such that 1 L = 1 dm³, 1 L = 1000 mL, 1 mL = 1 cm³.

1.1 Physical Nature of Matter

1.1.1 Matter is made up of particles

For a long time, two schools of thought prevailed regarding the nature of matter. One school believed matter to be continuous like a block of wood, whereas, the other thought that matter was made up of particles like sand. Let us perform an activity to decide about the nature of matter — is it continuous or particulate?

Activity 1.1

  • Take a 100 mL beaker.
  • Fill half the beaker with water and mark the level of water.
  • Dissolve some salt/ sugar with the help of a glass rod.
  • Observe any change in water level.
  • What do you think has happened to the salt?
  • Where does it disappear?
  • Does the level of water change?

1.1.2 How small are these particles of matter?

Activity 1.2

  • Take 2–3 crystals of potassium permanganate and dissolve them in 100 mL of water.
  • Take out about 10 mL of this solution and put it into another 90 mL of clear water.
  • Take out about 10 mL of this solution and put it into another 90 mL of clear water.
  • Keep diluting the solution like this 5 to 8 times.
  • Is the water still coloured?

This activity shows that just a few crystals of potassium permanganate can colour a large volume of water. So we conclude that there must be millions of tiny particles in just one crystal of potassium permanganate, which keep on dividing themselves into smaller and smaller particles, ultimately a stage may come when the particles cannot divide further into smaller particles.

1.2 Characteristics of Particles of Matter

1.2.1 Particles of matter have space between them

In Activity 1.1, the particles of salt get into the spaces between particles of water. This suggests that there is enough space between particles of matter — particles of matter have space between them.

1.2.2 Particles of matter are continuously moving

Activity 1.3

  • Put an unlit incense stick in a corner of the class. How close do you have to get near it so as to get its smell?
  • Now light the incense stick. What happens? Do you get the smell sitting at a distance?
  • Record your observations.

Activity 1.4

  • Take two glasses/beakers filled with water.
  • Put a drop of blue or red ink slowly and carefully along the sides of the first beaker and honey in the same way in the second beaker.
  • Leave them undisturbed in your house or in a corner of the class.
  • Record your observations.
  • What do you observe immediately after adding the ink?
  • What do you observe immediately after adding honey?
  • How many hours or days does it take for the colour of ink to spread evenly throughout the water?

The intermixing of particles of two different types of matter on their own is called diffusion.

Particles of matter are continuously moving, that is, they possess what we call the kinetic energy. As the temperature rises, particles move faster. So, we can say that with increase in temperature the kinetic energy of the particles also increases. The smell of hot sizzling food reaches you in a few seconds; compare it with the smell of cold food — you have to go close to get its smell.

Activity 1.5

  • Take two glasses of water, one with cold water and the other with hot water.
  • Put a drop of ink in each glass.
  • Do you observe any difference in how fast the ink spreads in the two glasses?

1.2.3 Particles of matter attract each other

Activity 1.6

  • Play this game in the field: make four groups and form human chains.
  • The first group should hold each other from the back and lock their hands.
  • The second group should hold hands to form a chain.
  • The third group should form a chain by touching each other with only their fingertips.
  • The fourth group of students should run around and try to break the three human chains, one by one, into as many small groups as possible.
  • Which group was the easiest to break? Why?
  • If we consider each student as a particle of matter, then in which group the particles held each other with the maximum force?
  • And, in which group, the particles of matter attracted each other with minimum force?

Activity 1.7

  • Take an iron nail, a piece of chalk and a rubber band.
  • Try breaking them by hammering, cutting or stretching.
  • Which of the above substances are you able to break easily? Describe the force of attraction between particles in each case.

Activity 1.8

  • Take some water in a container. Try breaking the surface of water with your fingers.
  • Were you able to break the surface of water?
  • What could be the reason for the surface of water remaining together?

These activities suggest the following: (a) Particles of matter have force of attraction between them. This force keeps the particles together. (b) The strength of forces of attraction varies from one kind of matter to another.

1.3 States of Matter

The matter around us exists in three different states — solid, liquid and gas. These states of matter arise due to the variation in the characteristics of the particles of matter.

The states of matter are inter-convertible. The state of matter can be changed by changing temperature or pressure. We will learn about this in the later sections of this chapter.

1.3.1 The solid state

Activity 1.9

  • Collect the following: a pen, a book, a needle and a piece of wooden stick.
  • Sketch the shape of these objects in your notebook. Do all these have a definite shape, distinct boundaries and a fixed volume?
  • What happens if they are hammered, pulled or dropped?
  • Are these capable of being compressed? Try compressing them by applying force. Are you able to compress them?

All the above are examples of solids. We observe that these: have a definite shape, distinct boundaries and a fixed volume, that is, negligible compressibility; have a tendency to maintain their shape when subjected to outside force; may break under force but it is difficult to change their shape, so they are rigid.

Consider a rubber band, can it change its shape on stretching? Is it a solid? A rubber band changes shape under force and regains the same shape when the force is removed. If excessive force is applied, it breaks. Each individual crystal of sugar or salt has a definite shape, no matter whether you take them in your hand or put them in a plate or jar.

1.3.2 The liquid state

Activity 1.10

  • Collect the following: (a) water, cooking oil, milk, juice, a cold drink. (b) containers of different shapes. Put a 50 mL mark on these containers using a measuring cylinder.
  • What will happen if these liquids are spilt on the floor?
  • Measure 50 mL of a liquid and transfer it into different containers one by one. Does the volume remain the same?
  • Does the shape of the liquid remain the same?
  • When you pour 50 mL of liquid from one container into another, does it flow easily?

Observe that liquids: have no fixed shape but have a fixed volume; take up the shape of the container in which they are kept; flow and change shape, so they are not rigid but can be called fluid.

1.3.3 The gaseous state

Activity 1.11

  • Take three 100 mL syringes and close their nozzles by inserting rubber corks in them.
  • Remove the pistons from all the syringes.
  • Leaving one syringe untouched, fill water in the second and pieces of chalk in the third.
  • Insert the pistons back into the syringes.
  • Now, try to compress the content by pushing the piston in each syringe.
  • What do you observe? In which case was the piston easily pushed in?
  • What do you infer from your observations?

We observe that gases are highly compressible as compared to solids and liquids. The liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) cylinder that we get in our home for cooking or the oxygen supplied to hospitals in cylinders is compressed gas. Compressed natural gas (CNG) is used as fuel these days in vehicles. Due to its high compressibility, large volumes of a gas can be compressed into a small cylinder and transported easily.

In the gaseous state, the particles move about randomly at high speed. Due to this random movement, the particles hit each other and also the walls of the container. The pressure exerted by the gas is because of this force exerted by gas particles per unit area on the walls of the container.

Q. Comment upon the following: rigidity, compressibility, fluidity, filling a gas container, shape, kinetic energy and density.

How to write this answer
  1. Take each word one by one, in the order given. One or two lines on each — what it means, and which state it belongs to.
  2. Start each part with the word itself, so the examiner can follow your answer down the list.

Rigidity — the property of solids to keep their shape when force is applied.

Compressibility — the decrease in volume when pressure is applied. It is highest in gases and almost nil in solids.

Fluidity — the ability to flow. Liquids and gases are fluids; solids are not.

Filling a gas container — gas particles move in all directions, so a gas spreads out and fills the whole container it is kept in.

Shape — only solids have a definite shape of their own.

Kinetic energy — the energy of moving particles. It is least in solids, more in liquids and maximum in gases, and it increases with temperature.

Density — mass per unit volume. It is generally highest in solids and lowest in gases.

Q. Give reasons: (a) A gas fills completely the vessel in which it is kept. (b) A gas exerts pressure on the walls of the container. (c) A wooden table should be called a solid. (d) We can easily move our hand in air, but to do the same through a solid block of wood we would need a karate expert.

1.4 Can Matter Change its State?

1.4.1 Effect of change of temperature

Activity 1.12

  • Take about 150 g of ice in a beaker.
  • Place a laboratory thermometer so that the bulb is thoroughly in contact with ice.
  • Start heating the beaker on a low flame.
  • Note the temperature when the ice starts melting.
  • Note the temperature when all the ice has melted into water.
  • Heat until the water starts boiling. Note the temperature when most of the water vaporises.
  • Record your observations for this activity and draw your conclusions.

On increasing the temperature of solids, the kinetic energy of the particles increases. Due to the increase in kinetic energy, the particles start vibrating with greater speed. The energy supplied by heat overcomes the forces of attraction between the particles. The particles leave their fixed positions and start moving more freely. A stage is reached when the solid melts and is converted to a liquid. The minimum temperature at which a solid melts to become a liquid at the atmospheric pressure is called its melting point.

The melting point of ice is 273.15 K. The process of melting, that is, change of solid state into liquid state, is also known as fusion.

When a solid melts, its temperature remains the same, so where does the heat energy go? This heat gets used up in changing the state by overcoming the forces of attraction between the particles. As this heat energy is absorbed by ice without showing any rise in temperature, it is considered that it gets hidden into the contents of the beaker and is known as the latent heat. The word latent means hidden. The amount of heat energy that is required to change 1 kg of a solid into liquid at atmospheric pressure at its melting point is known as the latent heat of fusion. So, latent heat of fusion of ice is 3.34 × 105 J/kg.

The temperature at which a liquid boils and is converted to vapour at atmospheric pressure is called its boiling point. Boiling point of water is 373 K (100 °C). The amount of heat energy that is required to change 1 kg of a liquid into vapour at atmospheric pressure at its boiling point is known as the latent heat of vaporisation. So, the latent heat of vaporisation of water is 22.5 × 105 J/kg.

Particles in steam, that is, water vapour at 373 K (100 °C) have more energy than water at the same temperature. This is because particles in steam have absorbed extra energy in the form of latent heat of vaporisation.

To change a temperature on the kelvin scale to the Celsius scale you have to subtract 273 from the given temperature, and to convert a temperature on the Celsius scale to the kelvin scale you have to add 273 to the given temperature.

  • Put an inverted funnel over the china dish.

We find that solid directly changes into gaseous state and vice versa without changing into liquid state. The change of solid directly into vapour is called sublimation, whereas, the reverse process, that is, the change of vapour into solid, is called deposition.

1.4.2 Effect of change of pressure

Applying pressure and reducing temperature can liquefy gases. Solid carbon dioxide (CO2) is stored under high pressure. Solid CO2 gets directly converted into gaseous state on decrease of pressure to 1 atmosphere without coming into liquid state. This is the reason that solid carbon dioxide is also known as dry ice. Thus, pressure and temperature determine the state of a substance.

Two more states of matter

Plasma: The state consists of super energetic and super excited particles. These particles are in the form of ionised gases. The fluorescent tube and neon sign bulbs consist of plasma. The gas gets ionised, that is, gets charged when electrical energy flows through it. This charging up creates a plasma glowing inside the tube or bulb. The Sun and the stars glow because of the presence of plasma in them. The plasma is created in stars because of very high temperature.

Bose–Einstein condensate: In 1920, Indian physicist Satyendra Nath Bose had done some calculations for a fifth state of matter. Building on his calculations, Albert Einstein predicted a new state of matter — the Bose–Einstein condensate (BEC). The BEC is formed by cooling a gas of extremely low density (about one-hundred-thousandth the density of normal air) to super-low temperatures.

Why this matters

While ice melts or water boils, the temperature does not rise, even though heat is going in. That “hidden” heat — latent heat — is the answer to four different questions in this chapter, including why steam burns worse than boiling water.

You see this in real life

Ice in your cold drink keeps it at a steady cold temperature while it melts, because all the heat it takes goes into melting, not into warming up.

Remember this for the exam

Two numbers, always with the unit: latent heat of fusion of ice = 3.34 × 105 J/kg; latent heat of vaporisation of water = 22.5 × 105 J/kg. And: K = °C + 273.

1.5 Evaporation

We are now aware of the fact that the particles of the liquid at the surface which have higher kinetic energy are able to overcome the forces of attraction of particles and escape from the liquid in the form of vapour. The phenomenon of change of a liquid into vapours at any temperature below its boiling point is called evaporation.

1.5.1 Factors affecting evaporation

Activity 1.14

  • Take 5 mL of water in a test tube and keep it near a window or under a fan.
  • Take 5 mL of water in an uncovered china dish and keep it near a window or under a fan.
  • Take 5 mL of water in an uncovered china dish and keep it inside a cupboard or under a table, shaded from all sides.
  • Record your observations daily for 2–3 days.
  • In which condition does the water evaporate faster?
  • On which day — hot or humid — does the water evaporate faster?

The rate of evaporation depends upon the surface area exposed to the atmosphere, the temperature, the humidity and the wind speed.

  1. Surface area: Evaporation is a surface phenomenon. If the surface area is increased, the rate of evaporation increases. For example, while putting clothes for drying up we spread them out.
  2. Temperature: With the increase of temperature, more number of particles get enough kinetic energy to go into the vapour state.
  3. Humidity: Humidity is the amount of water vapour present in air. The air around us cannot hold more than a definite amount of water vapour at a given temperature. If the amount of water in air is already high, the rate of evaporation decreases.
  4. Wind speed: It is observed that clothes dry faster on a windy day. With the increase in wind speed, the particles of water vapour move away with the wind, decreasing the amount of water vapour in the surrounding.

1.5.2 How does evaporation cause cooling?

In an open vessel, the liquid keeps on evaporating. The particles of liquid absorb energy from the surrounding to regain the energy lost during evaporation. This absorption of energy from the surroundings makes the surroundings cold.

Why this matters

Four of the five questions on page 10 of your book — the desert cooler, the matka, acetone on the palm, tea in a saucer — are all this one idea: evaporation takes heat from whatever the liquid touches, so that thing becomes cooler.

You see this in real life

Wet clothes dry fastest spread out on a sunny, windy day — large surface area, high temperature, moving air. On a rainy day they stay damp: the air is already full of water vapour.

Remember this for the exam

The four factors, with the direction: surface area ↑ faster · temperature ↑ faster · humidity ↑ slower · wind speed ↑ faster. Humidity works the other way — that is the line students most often write wrong.

Q. What produces more severe burns: boiling water or steam?

How to write this answer
  1. Answer in the first word: steam. Then give the reason — the extra latent heat it carries.

Steam produces more severe burns. Steam carries extra heat as the latent heat of vaporisation (22.5 × 105 J/kg), which boiling water does not have. When steam touches the skin, it first condenses and gives out this extra heat, and then the hot water formed acts on the skin too. So steam gives more heat to the skin than boiling water, and the burns are more severe.

Mistakes that quietly cost marks in this chapter

  • “Liquids have a fixed shape.” They don't — fixed volume, shape of the container. This exact mix-up is asked again and again in MCQs and case questions.
  • Treating evaporation and boiling as the same thing. Surface vs bulk, all temperatures vs one fixed temperature — if a question says “at all temperatures”, it is evaporation.
  • Forgetting to convert before comparing temperatures, and dropping units (J/kg, K) from latent-heat and conversion answers.

  • Writing that temperature rises during melting. During a change of state, supplied heat becomes latent heat — the thermometer does not move.
  • Assertion–reason: picking “R is the correct explanation” just because both statements are true. Test the “because” link.

How a brilliant student puts it on paper

These are the habits seen again and again in the answer copies that get full marks — in CBSE's own published model answer sheets and in the copies of students who scored the highest marks, all across India. None of them is about beautiful handwriting. All of them make it easy for the person checking to give you the mark.

  • Question number in the left margin, written before the answer starts.
  • Definition first, in one line, when the question has a term in it — and the key words in it underlined.
  • Points in order, numbered — one point per mark. A 3-mark answer shows 3 clear points, not one long paragraph.
  • Key words underlined as you write — surface, diffusion, latent heat, kinetic energy.
  • Diagram in the centre of the page, in pencil, just after the point it belongs to, with labels on the side.
  • For a numerical: formula, then the numbers put in, then the final answer boxed, with its unit. Each step carries its own mark, so a slip in the last step does not lose the earlier marks.
  • A blank line between answers. Length follows the marks: 2 marks ≈ 30–40 words, 3 marks ≈ 50–70 words, 5 marks ≈ 100–120 words.

Q1. Define matter. In which three states does it exist around us? Give one example of each.

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space (that is, has volume).

Matter around us exists in three states:

  1. Solid — has a definite shape and definite volume. Example: wood, ice.
  2. Liquid — has a definite volume but no definite shape; it takes the shape of its container. Example: water, milk.
  3. Gas — has neither definite shape nor definite volume; it fills the whole container. Example: air, steam.

  • 2 marks — the three states with a correct property or example for each (roughly ½ mark per state, ½ for the examples set).
Common mistake: defining matter as “anything we can see or touch.” Air is invisible and is still matter — and light fails the test completely because it has no mass. If your definition would include light, it is wrong.

Q2. State the three characteristics of particles of matter. Describe an activity to support each characteristic.

(1) Particles of matter have space between them. Activity: Take a beaker, fill it with water and mark the level. Dissolve salt in it with stirring. The salt disappears but the water level does not rise — the salt particles occupy the spaces between the water particles.

(2) Particles of matter are continuously moving. Activity: Light an incense stick in a corner of the room. Its fragrance reaches every part of the room within minutes, because its particles diffuse and mix with the moving particles of air.

(3) Particles of matter attract each other. Evidence: the force of attraction is strongest in solids — an iron nail keeps its shape and is hard to break; it is weaker in liquids and weakest in gases, which is why gases spread out freely.

Supporting activity (particles are very small): dissolve 2–3 crystals of potassium permanganate in 100 mL of water and dilute it repeatedly, 8–10 times. The solution remains coloured, showing that a few crystals contain millions of tiny particles.

Draw the dilution diagram HERE — after the supporting-activity paragraph, in the centre of the page, in pencil: 4 beakers in a row, colour fading, one label under each beaker.
Why this answer gets full marks
  • 1 mark — all three characteristics named correctly.
  • 3 marks — one valid activity or evidence per characteristic (1 each).
  • 1 mark — each activity ends by stating what it proves; the inference earns its own mark, separate from the description.
Common mistake: describing the activity beautifully and never writing the conclusion. Examiners tick “proves particles have space between them” — not the stirring. Always finish an activity answer with “…this shows that…”.

Q4. The temperature of ice kept in a beaker stays at 0 °C for some time even though it is being heated continuously. Explain why, with the help of a heating curve.

While ice is melting, all the heat supplied is used to overcome the force of attraction between the particles — it changes the state instead of raising the kinetic energy of the particles. This heat, taken up without any rise in temperature, is the latent heat of fusion. Since temperature depends on the kinetic energy of the particles, the thermometer stays at 0 °C (273 K) until every bit of ice has melted. Only then does the temperature start rising again.

In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the margin → explanation paragraph first (heat → attraction → latent heat) → draw the heating curve HERE, after the paragraph, in the centre of the page, in pencil: axes labelled, both flat stretches shaded and named.
Why this answer gets full marks
  • 1 mark — heat is used to overcome interparticle attraction (change of state).
  • 1 mark — naming it: latent heat; kinetic energy unchanged, so temperature unchanged.
  • 1 mark — the labelled heating curve with the plateau marked.
Common mistake: “The heat is lost” or “the thermometer takes time.” Heat is never lost here — it is stored as latent heat, which is exactly why the melted water at 0 °C has more energy than the ice did.

Q5. What is meant by latent heat? State the latent heat of fusion of ice and the latent heat of vaporisation of water.

Latent heat is the amount of heat energy required to change the state of 1 kg of a substance without any change in its temperature. It is called “latent” (hidden) because the temperature does not rise while it is being absorbed.

Latent heat of fusion of ice = 3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg — the heat needed to convert 1 kg of ice at 0 °C into water at 0 °C.

Latent heat of vaporisation of water = 22.5 × 10⁵ J/kg — the heat needed to convert 1 kg of water at 100 °C into steam at 100 °C.

In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the margin → definition first, with “1 kg” and “without change in temperature” underlined → the two values as two separate lines, each with its unit J/kg boxed. No diagram for this question.
Why this answer gets full marks
  • 1 mark — definition carrying both key phrases: “1 kg” and “without change in temperature.”
  • 1 mark each — each value with its unit, J/kg. A value without a unit is an incomplete answer.
Common mistake: swapping the two values, or writing them per gram. Vaporisation (22.5 × 10⁵) is far bigger than fusion (3.34 × 10⁵) — breaking particles completely apart takes much more energy than just loosening them. Remember it by logic, not by rote.

Q6. Why does steam cause more severe burns than boiling water, though both are at 100 °C?

Steam contains extra energy in the form of the latent heat of vaporisation (22.5 × 10⁵ J/kg), which boiling water does not have. When steam touches the skin, it first condenses and releases this extra heat, and then the heat of the hot water formed acts on the skin as well. So steam delivers more heat to the skin than the same amount of boiling water, and the burns are more severe.

In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the margin → line 1: steam carries latent heat → line 2: it condenses on skin and releases it → line 3: conclusion. Two marks, three lines — no diagram, no introduction.
Why this answer gets full marks
  • 1 mark — steam carries latent heat of vaporisation in addition to its heat at 100 °C.
  • 1 mark — on contact it condenses, releasing that extra heat onto the skin.
Common mistake: “Steam is hotter than boiling water.” At the same pressure both are at 100 °C — the difference is not temperature, it is stored latent heat. Writing “hotter” loses the first mark immediately.

Q7. Ice at 273 K is more effective in cooling a drink than water at 273 K. Why?

Both are at the same temperature, but ice can absorb extra heat from the drink as the latent heat of fusion (3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg) to convert itself into water at 273 K. Water at 273 K can only take heat by warming up. So ice removes more heat from the drink and cools it more effectively.

In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the margin → line 1: ice absorbs latent heat of fusion from the drink → line 2: water at 273 K cannot → conclusion in the same line. No diagram.
Why this answer gets full marks
  • 1 mark — ice absorbs latent heat of fusion from the surroundings (the drink) in order to melt.
  • 1 mark — water at the same temperature has no such extra heat absorption, so it cools less.
Common mistake: “Ice is colder than water.” Both are at 273 K — identical temperature. The examiner is testing whether you reach for latent heat instead of the word “colder.”

Q8. What is evaporation? State and explain the factors which affect the rate of evaporation.

Evaporation is the change of a liquid into its vapour from its surface, taking place at all temperatures. Its rate depends on four factors:

  1. Surface area — evaporation is a surface phenomenon, so a larger exposed surface lets more particles escape. Water dries faster in a plate than in a bottle.
  2. Temperature — at higher temperature, more particles have enough kinetic energy to escape, so evaporation increases. Clothes dry faster in the sun.
  3. Humidity — if the air already contains a large amount of water vapour, it can hold less more, so the rate of evaporation decreases. Clothes dry slowly on a humid day.
  4. Wind speed — wind carries the vapour particles away from the surface, so evaporation increases. Clothes dry faster on a windy day.
In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the margin → definition first (surface + all temperatures underlined) → four numbered factors, name of factor in bold at the start of each line, direction of effect, one example each → stop at factor 4. No diagram earns marks here.
Why this answer gets full marks
  • 1 mark — definition with both key phrases: “from the surface” and “at all temperatures.”
  • 1 mark per factor — the factor named and the direction of its effect explained; an everyday example is the safest way to show the direction.
Common mistake: listing the four factors with no explanation — that earns half marks at best. And for humidity, the direction is where students slip: more humidity → slower evaporation, not faster.

Q10. Why does the water kept in an earthen pot (matka) become cool in summer?

An earthen pot is porous. Water from inside keeps seeping out through the pores to the outer surface, where it evaporates. For evaporation, the water takes the required latent heat from the water stored inside the pot. The stored water therefore loses heat, its temperature falls, and it becomes cool.

In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the margin → mechanism as a 3-step chain: pores → evaporation at the outer surface → latent heat taken from the stored water. Draw the matka diagram HERE — after the written chain, centre of the page, in pencil, with exactly 3 labels: pores, water seeps out, evaporation cools the water inside.
Why this answer gets full marks
  • 1 mark — porous pot: water seeps through pores and evaporates from the outer surface.
  • 1 mark — the evaporating water takes its latent heat from the stored water, so the stored water cools.
Common mistake: “Earthen pots are bad conductors, so the water stays cool.” Conductivity is not the mechanism — a steel pot with the same water does not cool it. If your answer never says the word evaporation, it scores zero here.

Q11. (a) Convert 293 K and 470 K to the Celsius scale. (b) Convert 25 °C and 373 °C to the Kelvin scale.

The relation between the scales is:

Temperature in K = Temperature in °C + 273  ·  °C = K − 273

(a) 293 K = 293 − 273 = 20 °C  ·  470 K = 470 − 273 = 197 °C

(b) 25 °C = 25 + 273 = 298 K  ·  373 °C = 373 + 273 = 646 K

Why this answer gets full marks
  • 1 mark — the relation written once, correctly, before any numbers.
  • ½ mark per conversion — substitution shown, correct answer, correct unit. Kelvin answers carry no degree symbol: write 298 K, never 298 °K.

Q. “We are advised to wear cotton clothes in summer.” Justify this statement by explaining the cooling caused by evaporation. Give two more examples of evaporation causing cooling in daily life. (5 marks)
Evaporation and cooling: Evaporation is the change of a liquid into vapour from its surface at all temperatures. The particles at the surface that escape are those with the highest kinetic energy, so the average kinetic energy of the particles left behind decreases. As a result, the temperature of the liquid falls — evaporation causes cooling.
1 markDefinition + the mechanism. Both halves needed: naming the process alone earns nothing here.
Why cotton in summer: In summer our body produces more sweat. Cotton is a good absorber of water — it soaks up the sweat and exposes it to the atmosphere over a large surface area. The sweat then evaporates easily, taking the required latent heat from our body, and we feel cool and comfortable.
2 marksApplies the mechanism to the exact situation asked: absorb → expose → evaporate → body loses heat. One mark for the chain, one for linking it back to the statement.
Two more examples: (1) Water kept in an earthen pot (matka) stays cool, because water seeps through its pores and evaporates from the outer surface. (2) A desert cooler cools a room on a hot, dry day, because the water in its pads evaporates continuously and takes heat from the air passing through.
2 marksOne mark per example — and each example still names the mechanism instead of just naming the object.

Notice what is not in that answer: no introduction paragraph, no conclusion, no repeated definition. Five marks, five clear points, key words underlined, done. Length was never the goal — clear points the examiner can tick are.

Give reasons: (a) A gas fills completely the vessel in which it is kept. (b) A gas exerts pressure on the walls of its container. (c) The smell of perfume reaches us even when we are sitting several metres away.

  • (a) Gas particles move rapidly in all directions and interparticle attraction is negligible, so they spread to fill the whole available space — 1 mark.
  • (b) Particles move randomly at high speed and collide with the container walls; these collisions exert pressure — 1 mark.
  • (c) Perfume particles are continuously moving and diffuse into air, spreading in all directions until they reach us — 1 mark. Key word the examiner looks for: diffusion.

Q. With the help of an activity, show that gases are more easily compressible than liquids and solids.

Activity: Take three 100 mL syringes and close their nozzles with rubber corks. Remove the pistons; leave one syringe empty (air), fill the second with water and the third with pieces of chalk. Q. What is evaporation? Explain in detail the factors affecting the rate of evaporation.

Evaporation is the phenomenon of change of a liquid into vapours at any temperature below its boiling point, taking place from the surface of the liquid. The rate of evaporation depends on four factors:

  1. Surface area — evaporation is a surface phenomenon; the larger the surface exposed, the faster the evaporation. Water dries faster in a plate than in a bottle.
  2. Temperature — at higher temperature, more particles have enough kinetic energy to escape into the vapour state, so evaporation increases.
  3. Humidity — humidity is the amount of water vapour already present in air. If humidity is high, the air accepts vapour slowly and the rate of evaporation decreases. Clothes dry slowly in the monsoon.
  4. Wind speed — wind carries vapour particles away from above the surface, so evaporation increases. Clothes dry faster on a windy day.

Q. What type of clothes should we wear in summer, and why?

We should wear cotton clothes in summer. During summer we perspire more. Cotton is a good absorber of water — it absorbs the sweat and exposes it to the atmosphere over a large surface area. The sweat evaporates easily, taking the heat equal to the latent heat of vaporisation from the body, so the body feels cool and comfortable.
