Step 1 · Textbook as is · NCERT pp. 1–3

1.1 Physical Nature of Matter

Everything around us is made up of material called matter — the air we breathe, food, clouds, stars, plants, animals, water, soil, even the smallest drop of water or particle of sand. Matter occupies space and has mass. Volume is measured in litres: 1 L = 1 dm³, 1 L = 1000 mL, 1 mL = 1 cm³; the SI unit of volume is the cubic metre (m³).

Early thinkers in India and Greece classified matter into five basic elements — the panch tatva: air, earth, fire, sky and water. Modern scientists developed two schools of thought: that matter is continuous, like a block of wood, or that it is particulate, made of tiny particles. Matter is particulate.

1.1.2 How small are these particles?

Activity 1.1: Dissolve 2–3 crystals of potassium permanganate in 100 mL of water. Take out about 10 mL and make it up to 100 mL again. Repeat the dilution again and again — even after many dilutions, the water remains coloured. Conclusion: just a few crystals of potassium permanganate can colour a large volume of water, so there must be millions of tiny particles in one crystal, which keep dividing at every dilution. Particles of matter are very, very small.

1.2 Characteristics of the Particles of Matter

1.2.1 Particles of matter have space between them

Activity 1.2: Fill a beaker with water to a marked level and dissolve salt or sugar in it with stirring. The salt disappears and the water level does not rise — the particles of salt occupy the spaces between the particles of water. A diver can cut through water for the same reason: there are spaces between the particles of a liquid.

1.2.2 Particles of matter are continuously moving

Activity 1.3: Put an unlit incense stick in a corner — its smell is caught only close by. Light it, and the smell reaches the other end of the room. The intermixing of particles of two different types of matter on their own is called diffusion. Particles keep moving because they possess kinetic energy; with a rise in temperature the kinetic energy increases, so diffusion becomes faster — the smell of hot sizzling food travels further than the smell of cold food.

1.2.3 Particles of matter attract each other

Activity 1.4: Try to break an iron nail, a piece of chalk and a rubber band by pulling, hammering or cutting. The force needed is very different in each case, because the force of attraction between particles is different in different substances — strongest in solids, weaker in liquids and weakest in gases.

NCERT in-text questions — page 3, with answers

Q1. Which of the following are matter? Chair, air, love, smell, hate, almonds, thought, cold, cold drink, smell of perfume.

Chair, air, almonds and cold drink are matter — each has mass and occupies space. The smell of perfume reaches us because tiny perfume particles (matter) diffuse through air. Love, hate, thought, cold and smell as feelings or sensations have no mass and occupy no space, so they are not matter.

Q2. Give a reason: the smell of hot sizzling food reaches you several metres away, but to get the smell from cold food you have to go close.

Particles of matter are continuously moving and their kinetic energy increases with temperature. In hot food the particles diffuse into air much faster and travel further; in cold food diffusion is slow, so the smell is felt only nearby.

Q3. A diver is able to cut through water in a swimming pool. Which property of matter does this observation show?

It shows that particles of matter have space between them. The spaces between water particles let the diver pass through.

Q4. What are the characteristics of the particles of matter?

(1) Particles of matter have space between them. (2) Particles of matter are continuously moving. (3) Particles of matter attract each other.

1.3 States of Matter

Matter around us exists in three different states — solid, liquid and gas. The states are inter-convertible; the state can be changed by changing temperature or pressure.

The solid state

Solids have a definite shape, distinct boundaries and a fixed volume. They tend to maintain their shape when subjected to outside force and are rigid — a solid may break under force, but its shape is difficult to change. Neglecting the small exceptions (a rubber band changes shape under force and regains it; sugar and salt take the shape of the container but each crystal is a solid), solids are almost incompressible.

The liquid state

Liquids have no fixed shape but a fixed volume. They take up the shape of the container in which they are kept and can flow — liquids are fluid. Many liquids and gases are stored and transported in solid containers.

The gaseous state

Gases are highly compressible as compared to solids and liquids. It is because of this property that large volumes of gas can be kept in a small cylinder and transported easily — liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) for domestic use, oxygen cylinders in hospitals, and compressed natural gas (CNG) in vehicles. Because of high speed and large spaces between particles, gases show the property of diffusion very fast. A gas fills the container completely and exerts pressure on its walls, because its particles move randomly at high speed and collide with the walls.

NCERT in-text questions — page 6, with answers

Q1. Density = mass/volume. Arrange in increasing density: air, exhaust from chimney, honey, water, chalk, cotton, iron.

Air < exhaust from chimney < cotton < water < honey < chalk < iron.

Q2(a). Tabulate the differences in the characteristics of the states of matter.

Solid: definite shape and volume, incompressible, rigid, cannot flow. Liquid: no definite shape (takes the container's shape), definite volume, slightly compressible, can flow. Gas: neither definite shape nor volume, highly compressible, can flow in all directions. Force of attraction: maximum in solids, less in liquids, least in gases. Kinetic energy: least in solids, more in liquids, maximum in gases.

Q2(b). Comment on: rigidity, compressibility, fluidity, filling a gas container, shape, kinetic energy, density.

Rigidity — the property of a solid to resist change of shape. Compressibility — decrease in volume on applying pressure; high for gases, negligible for solids. Fluidity — the ability to flow; liquids and gases are fluids, solids are not. Filling a gas container — gas particles move freely in all directions, so a gas spreads to fill the whole container. Shape — definite only for solids. Kinetic energy — energy of motion of particles; increases from solid to liquid to gas and with temperature. Density — mass per unit volume; generally highest in solids, lowest in gases.

Q3. Give reasons: (a) A gas fills completely the vessel in which it is kept. (b) A gas exerts pressure on the walls of the container. (c) A wooden table should be called a solid. (d) We can easily move our hand in air, but to do the same through a solid block of wood we would need a karate expert.

(a) The force of attraction between gas particles is negligible and they move rapidly in all directions, so they spread through the whole vessel. (b) Gas particles move randomly at high speed and collide with each other and with the walls; these collisions exert pressure. (c) A wooden table has a definite shape and a definite volume — the two defining properties of a solid. (d) Air particles have large spaces between them and almost no attraction, so a hand passes easily; wood particles are tightly packed with strong attraction and the block is rigid, so it resists.

Q4. Liquids generally have lower density than solids. But ice floats on water. Why?

When water freezes, its particles arrange themselves with large vacant spaces between them in the ice structure. So the same mass of ice occupies more volume than water — ice has a lower density than water, and it floats.

Step 1 · Textbook as is · NCERT pp. 7–9

1.4 Can Matter Change Its State?

Effect of change of temperature

On increasing the temperature of solids, the kinetic energy of the particles increases. The particles vibrate with greater speed and the heat overcomes the force of attraction between them; at a certain temperature the solid melts and is converted into liquid. The temperature at which a solid melts to become a liquid at atmospheric pressure is its melting point. The melting point of ice is 273.15 K.

The process of melting — change of solid state into liquid state — is also known as fusion. While ice melts, the temperature stays constant: the heat supplied is used up in changing the state, in overcoming the forces of attraction. The amount of heat energy required to change 1 kg of a solid into liquid at its melting point, at atmospheric pressure, is the latent heat of fusion. Particles of water at 0 °C (273 K) therefore have more energy than particles of ice at the same temperature.

On heating a liquid, at a certain temperature the particles gain enough energy to change into the vapour state throughout the liquid — this is boiling, and the temperature is the boiling point (for water, 373 K). The heat energy required to change 1 kg of a liquid into vapour at its boiling point is the latent heat of vaporisation. Steam — water vapour at 373 K — carries more energy than boiling water at the same temperature. Some solids change directly into vapour without passing through the liquid state: this is sublimation (camphor, naphthalene, dry ice); the reverse change, vapour directly into solid, is deposition.

Effect of change of pressure

Applying pressure and reducing temperature can liquefy gases by bringing their particles closer. Solid carbon dioxide is stored under high pressure; it gets directly converted into the gaseous state on decrease of pressure to 1 atmosphere without coming into the liquid state — that is why solid CO2 is called dry ice.

Scientists now also speak of two more states: plasma — superheated, ionised gas (electrons and ions), present in the sun and stars and inside fluorescent tubes and neon sign tubes; and the Bose–Einstein condensate, formed by cooling a gas of extremely low density to super-low temperatures.

NCERT in-text questions — page 9, with answers

Q1. Convert to the Celsius scale: (a) 300 K (b) 573 K.

°C = K − 273. (a) 300 − 273 = 27 °C. (b) 573 − 273 = 300 °C.

Q2. What is the physical state of water at: (a) 250 °C (b) 100 °C?

(a) At 250 °C, well above the boiling point, water is in the gaseous state (steam). (b) At 100 °C water can exist in both liquid and gaseous states — it is boiling, changing from liquid to vapour at constant temperature.

Q3. For any substance, why does the temperature remain constant during the change of state?

The heat supplied is used to overcome the force of attraction between the particles — it becomes latent heat and changes the state instead of increasing the kinetic energy of the particles. Since temperature measures kinetic energy, it stays constant.

Q4. Suggest a method to liquefy atmospheric gases.

Bring the particles closer: increase the pressure and decrease the temperature. Under high pressure and low temperature the gas liquefies.

1.5 Evaporation

The phenomenon of change of a liquid into vapours at any temperature below its boiling point is called evaporation. In a liquid, a few particles at the surface have higher kinetic energy and overcome the forces of attraction of the other particles; they escape as vapour. Evaporation is a surface phenomenon; boiling is a bulk phenomenon that takes place at a fixed temperature.

Factors which affect evaporation

The rate of evaporation increases with: (1) an increase of surface area — evaporation is a surface phenomenon, so more exposed surface means faster evaporation; (2) an increase of temperature — more particles get enough kinetic energy to escape; (3) a decrease in humidity — humidity is the amount of water vapour already present in air, and air can hold only a definite amount, so humid air slows evaporation; (4) an increase in wind speed — wind carries the vapour away, decreasing the amount of vapour around and speeding evaporation.

How does evaporation cause cooling?

The particles of liquid absorb energy from the surroundings to regain the energy lost during evaporation — heat equal to the latent heat of vaporisation is taken from whatever the liquid touches, leaving it cool. That is why acetone or perfume on the palm feels cold (it evaporates faster than water), why water droplets appear on a tumbler of ice-cold water (water vapour in air loses energy on the cold surface and condenses), why people sprinkle water on a hot roof, and why cotton clothes suit summer: cotton absorbs sweat and exposes it to the atmosphere for easy evaporation, and the body loses heat and feels cool.

NCERT in-text questions — page 10, with answers

Q1. Why does a desert cooler cool better on a hot dry day?

On a hot dry day the temperature is high and the humidity is low, so water from the cooler pads evaporates faster. Faster evaporation takes more latent heat from the air passing through, so the cooling is better.

Q2. How does the water kept in an earthen pot (matka) become cool during summer?

The earthen pot is porous. Water seeps out through the pores and evaporates from the outer surface, taking its latent heat from the water stored inside. The stored water loses heat and becomes cool.

Q3. Why does our palm feel cold when we put some acetone, petrol or perfume on it?

These liquids evaporate very fast. To evaporate, their particles take the required energy (latent heat) from the palm. The palm loses heat, its temperature falls, and it feels cold — colder than with water, which evaporates slowly.

Q4. Why are we able to sip hot tea or milk faster from a saucer rather than from a cup?

In a saucer the liquid spreads over a larger surface area, so it evaporates faster and cools faster than in a cup.

Q5. What type of clothes should we wear in summer?

Cotton clothes. Cotton is a good absorber of sweat; it absorbs the sweat and exposes it to the atmosphere, so it evaporates easily, takes latent heat from the body, and keeps us cool.

Step 1 · Textbook as is · NCERT Exercises, page 12

Exercises Q1–Q5, with answers

Q1. Convert the following temperatures to the Celsius scale: (a) 293 K (b) 470 K.

°C = K − 273. (a) 293 − 273 = 20 °C. (b) 470 − 273 = 197 °C.

Q2. Convert the following temperatures to the Kelvin scale: (a) 25 °C (b) 373 °C.

K = °C + 273. (a) 25 + 273 = 298 K. (b) 373 + 273 = 646 K.

Q3. Give reasons: (a) Naphthalene balls disappear with time without leaving any solid. (b) We can get the smell of perfume sitting several metres away.

(a) Naphthalene undergoes sublimation — it changes directly from solid into vapour, so the balls shrink and vanish with no residue. (b) Perfume particles are continuously moving; they diffuse and intermix with air, spreading in all directions until they reach us.

Q4. Arrange the following substances in increasing order of force of attraction between their particles: water, sugar, oxygen.

Oxygen < water < sugar. Oxygen is a gas (weakest attraction), water a liquid, sugar a solid (strongest attraction).

Q5. What is the physical state of water at: (a) 25 °C (b) 0 °C (c) 100 °C?

(a) Liquid. (b) At 0 °C water can exist as solid (ice) and liquid together — it is the melting point. (c) At 100 °C water can exist as liquid and gas (steam) together — it is the boiling point.

Step 1 · Textbook as is · NCERT Exercises, page 12

Exercises Q6–Q9, with answers

Q6. Give two reasons to justify: (a) water at room temperature is a liquid. (b) an iron almirah is a solid at room temperature.

(a) Water at room temperature (i) has a definite volume and (ii) has no definite shape — it takes the shape of its container and flows. Those are the properties of a liquid. (b) An iron almirah (i) has a definite shape and definite volume and (ii) is rigid — the properties of a solid.

Q7. Why is ice at 273 K more effective in cooling than water at the same temperature?

Ice at 273 K absorbs extra heat from the surroundings as the latent heat of fusion (3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg) to melt into water at 273 K. Water at 273 K has already absorbed that heat, so it can cool only by warming up. Ice therefore removes more heat and cools more effectively.

Q8. What produces more severe burns — boiling water or steam?

Steam. Steam carries the extra latent heat of vaporisation (22.5 × 10⁵ J/kg) in addition to the heat of boiling water. On contact with skin it condenses and releases that extra heat first, so burns from steam are more severe.

Step 2 · Explain · Concepts

1 · Everything is made of tiny particles

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space (has volume). A book, air, water, your body — all matter. Light and heat are not matter: they have no mass and occupy no space. The big idea of this chapter is that matter is particulate — made of extremely small particles (atoms and molecules) — and almost everything in the chapter follows from three properties of those particles.

NCERT proves each property with an activity. These activities are favourite exam material, so learn what each one proves, not just what happens:

  • Activity 1.1 — the dilution that never ends. Dissolve 2–3 crystals of potassium permanganate in 100 mL of water. Take out 10 mL, dilute it to 100 mL again, and repeat 8–10 times. The water stays faintly coloured every single time. Proves: a few crystals contain millions of tiny particles, which keep dividing into smaller and smaller numbers — particles of matter are very, very small.
  • Activity 1.2 — the incense stick. An unlit incense stick can be smelled only up close. Light it, and the smell reaches the far corner of the room. Proves: particles of matter are continuously moving — they spread and mix with air on their own (this mixing is called diffusion).
  • Activity 1.3 — salt that vanishes without a trace. Mark the water level in a beaker, dissolve salt or sugar in it with stirring. The level does not rise. Proves: there are spaces between the particles of water, and the salt particles slip into those spaces.
The three properties — memorise this frame

Particles of matter (1) have space between them, (2) are continuously moving, and (3) attract each other. With temperature, the movement (kinetic energy) increases. Nearly every “give reason” question in this chapter is answered by pointing at one of these three.

Two more everyday proofs of movement: a drop of ink spreads through a whole glass of water, and the smell of cooking reaches another room. Diffusion happens fastest in gases, slower in liquids, and slowest in solids — two metal blocks pressed together for years join only slightly. And the force of attraction is not equal everywhere: it is strongest in solids (a nail holds its shape), weaker in liquids, and weakest in gases — which is exactly why the three states behave so differently.

Exam traps hidden in this table

Sugar and salt take the shape of the jar — but they are solids. Each individual crystal keeps its own definite shape; only the heap changes shape. Same logic: a rubber band changes shape under force but it is a solid, and a sponge can be compressed only because its pores hold trapped air.

Why does a gas fill the whole vessel? Its particles move rapidly in all directions and the attraction between them is negligible — so nothing holds them in one part of the container. Why does a gas press on the walls? The moving particles collide with the walls; that bombardment is pressure.

Step 2 · Explain · Change of state & evaporation

3 · Changing state — heat breaks the arrangement, not the particles

Heat a solid and its particles vibrate harder until they overcome the force of attraction holding them in place — the solid melts. Keep heating the liquid and particles gain enough energy to break free completely — it vaporises. Every change has a name, and the reverse change has its own name. Exams test both directions:

Melting point — the temperature at which a solid melts at atmospheric pressure. For ice it is 0 °C = 273.15 K. Boiling point of water is 100 °C = 373 K. While a change of state is happening, the temperature stays constant even though heat is still being supplied — that hidden heat has a name:

Latent heat — the two numbers to know cold

Latent heat is the heat absorbed (or released) during a change of state without any change in temperature — it is spent breaking (or forming) interparticle attractions. Latent heat of fusion of ice = 3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg. Latent heat of vaporisation of water = 22.5 × 10⁵ J/kg. That second, huge number is why steam causes far worse burns than boiling water: steam delivers its latent heat to your skin first, then the hot water's heat on top. It is also why water particles at 0 °C carry more energy than ice particles at 0 °C — the water has already absorbed the latent heat of fusion.

Temperature: the Kelvin scale

The SI unit of temperature is the kelvin (K). The scales are linked by one line — and at least one conversion question appears in almost every paper:

Temperature in K = Temperature in °C + 273
  • 25 °C → 25 + 273 = 298 K
  • 300 K → 300 − 273 = 27 °C
  • Which is hotter, 45 °C or 300 K? 45 °C = 318 K — so 45 °C is hotter. Always convert to one scale before comparing.

Pressure changes state too: increasing pressure and decreasing temperature can liquefy gases — that is how gases are stored as liquids in cylinders. Two more states exist beyond the familiar three: plasma — superheated, ionised gas found in the sun, stars, fluorescent tubes and neon sign tubes; and the Bose–Einstein condensate (BEC) — formed by cooling a gas of extremely low density to super-low temperatures, based on the work of Satyendra Nath Bose and Albert Einstein.

4 · Evaporation — the slow escape that cools everything it leaves

In any liquid, particles have a range of energies. A few at the surface have enough kinetic energy to break free — at any temperature. That is evaporation: a surface phenomenon. Boiling is different: it happens throughout the liquid, at one fixed temperature. This comparison is a guaranteed 2- or 3-marker:

EvaporationBoiling
Where it happensOnly at the surfaceThroughout the liquid (bulk phenomenon)
TemperatureAt all temperaturesOnly at the fixed boiling point
SpeedSlow and silentFast, with bubbling
Effect on the liquidCauses coolingTemperature stays at the boiling point

Four factors control the rate of evaporation — write them as a set, exams pick one and ask “why”:

  1. Surface area — more surface, more escaping particles. A saucer dries faster than a cup; spread clothes dry faster than folded ones.
  2. Temperature — hotter liquid, more high-energy particles, faster evaporation.
  3. Humidity — if the air already holds a lot of water vapour, evaporation slows down. Clothes dry slowly in the monsoon.
  4. Wind speed — wind carries vapour away, so evaporation speeds up. Clothes dry faster on a windy day.
Why evaporation cools — the single most-asked reasoning in this chapter

The particles that escape are the ones with the highest kinetic energy. So the average kinetic energy of the particles left behind falls — and temperature is a measure of that average energy. The liquid (and whatever it touches) becomes cooler. This one mechanism explains: water staying cool in an earthen pot (water seeps through the pores and evaporates from the outer surface), a desert cooler working best on a hot, dry day, acetone or perfume feeling colder than water on your palm (they evaporate faster), sprinkling water on a hot roof, and why cotton clothes suit summer — they absorb sweat and expose it to air so it can evaporate and cool you.

Step 4 · Written Answers · Layout & playbook
The answer-copy layout — copy this for every written answer

1. Write the question number in the left margin before you write anything else.

2. Definition first. If the question contains a term (evaporation, latent heat), its definition is line 1 — examiners tick it before reading further.

3. Points in order, one per line, numbered. Underline the keywords — surface, latent heat, kinetic energy, diffusion — as you write them.

4. Diagram placement: when a diagram earns marks, draw it in the centre of the page, in pencil, immediately after the point it belongs to (the exact spot is stated on every model answer in this step), with label lines drawn to the right and at least 4 labels. Never push a diagram to the end of the answer.

5. Numericals in a box: formula on the first line, substitution on the second, answer with unit on the third — each on its own line, inside a hand-drawn box.

6. Close with one line — an example or a conclusion that repeats the keyword. Leave one blank line before the next answer.

The playbook for each question type

MCQ (1 mark)

  • Eliminate the impossible options first — most questions fall to two.
  • Watch units and scale traps: 300 K is not hotter than 45 °C.
  • Never leave one blank — there is no negative marking.

Assertion–Reason (1 mark)

  • Judge A alone. Judge R alone. Only then ask: does R explain A?
  • The trap option is “both true” when R is true but explains nothing.
  • Say the link word in your head: “A happens because R.” If the sentence sounds wrong, R is not the explanation.

Case-based (4 marks)

  • Read the questions before the passage — you will spot the answers as you read.
  • Most sub-answers sit inside the passage; the last one usually applies the chapter concept to it.
  • Answer in the passage's own terms, then add the concept word (diffusion, evaporation…).

Short answer (2–3 marks)

  • Marks = value points. A 3-marker needs 3 distinct points, not one point written three ways.
  • One point per line or sentence — examiners tick what they can see.
  • For “give reason”: state the observation, then the particle-level cause.

Long answer (5 marks)

  • Open with the definition or principle. Then 4–5 headed or numbered points. Close with an example or a labelled diagram where it fits.
  • A comparison is always a table, never a paragraph.
  • Underline key terms. In numericals: formula → substitution → answer with unit, each step on its own line — step marking pays even if the final number slips.

Whole-paper habits

  • Attempt in section order, but park any question that stalls you — 80 marks in 180 minutes is roughly 2 minutes per mark.
  • Keep the last 10 minutes for units, conversions and un-attempted 1-markers.
  • Neat, spaced answers get read fully. Cramped ones get skimmed — and skimmed answers lose value points the examiner never saw.

Q1. Define matter. In which three states does it exist around us? Give one example of each.

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space (that is, has volume).

Matter around us exists in three states:

  1. Solid — has a definite shape and definite volume. Example: wood, ice.
  2. Liquid — has a definite volume but no definite shape; it takes the shape of its container. Example: water, milk.
  3. Gas — has neither definite shape nor definite volume; it fills the whole container. Example: air, steam.
In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the left margin → definition as the first line → the three states as numbered points, one property + one example each → stop. No diagram is asked in this question — do not spend time drawing one.
How the marks are given
  • 1 mark — the definition, and only if both conditions appear: mass and occupies space.
  • 2 marks — the three states with a correct property or example for each (roughly ½ mark per state, ½ for the examples set).
Common mistake: defining matter as “anything we can see or touch.” Air is invisible and is still matter — and light fails the test completely because it has no mass. If your definition would include light, it is wrong.

Q2. State the three characteristics of particles of matter. Describe an activity to support each characteristic.

(1) Particles of matter have space between them. Activity: Take a beaker, fill it with water and mark the level. Dissolve salt in it with stirring. The salt disappears but the water level does not rise — the salt particles occupy the spaces between the water particles.

(2) Particles of matter are continuously moving. Activity: Light an incense stick in a corner of the room. Its fragrance reaches every part of the room within minutes, because its particles diffuse and mix with the moving particles of air.

(3) Particles of matter attract each other. Evidence: the force of attraction is strongest in solids — an iron nail keeps its shape and is hard to break; it is weaker in liquids and weakest in gases, which is why gases spread out freely.

Supporting activity (particles are very small): dissolve 2–3 crystals of potassium permanganate in 100 mL of water and dilute it repeatedly, 8–10 times. The solution remains coloured, showing that a few crystals contain millions of tiny particles.

In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the margin → characteristic 1 with its activity → characteristic 2 with its activity → characteristic 3 with evidence → supporting activity last. Draw the dilution diagram HERE — after the supporting-activity paragraph, in the centre of the page, in pencil: 4 beakers in a row, colour fading, one label under each beaker.
How the marks are given
  • 1 mark — all three characteristics named correctly.
  • 3 marks — one valid activity or evidence per characteristic (1 each).
  • 1 mark — each activity ends by stating what it proves; the inference is a separate value point from the description.
Common mistake: describing the activity beautifully and never writing the conclusion. Examiners tick “proves particles have space between them” — not the stirring. Always finish an activity answer with “…this shows that…”.

Q4. The temperature of ice kept in a beaker stays at 0 °C for some time even though it is being heated continuously. Explain why, with the help of a heating curve.

While ice is melting, all the heat supplied is used to overcome the force of attraction between the particles — it changes the state instead of raising the kinetic energy of the particles. This heat, taken up without any rise in temperature, is the latent heat of fusion. Since temperature depends on the kinetic energy of the particles, the thermometer stays at 0 °C (273 K) until every bit of ice has melted. Only then does the temperature start rising again.

In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the margin → explanation paragraph first (heat → attraction → latent heat) → draw the heating curve HERE, after the paragraph, in the centre of the page, in pencil: axes labelled, both flat stretches shaded and named.
How the marks are given
  • 1 mark — heat is used to overcome interparticle attraction (change of state).
  • 1 mark — naming it: latent heat; kinetic energy unchanged, so temperature unchanged.
  • 1 mark — the labelled heating curve with the plateau marked.
Common mistake: “The heat is lost” or “the thermometer takes time.” Heat is never lost here — it is stored as latent heat, which is exactly why the melted water at 0 °C has more energy than the ice did.

Q5. What is meant by latent heat? State the latent heat of fusion of ice and the latent heat of vaporisation of water.

Latent heat is the amount of heat energy required to change the state of 1 kg of a substance without any change in its temperature. It is called “latent” (hidden) because the temperature does not rise while it is being absorbed.

Latent heat of fusion of ice = 3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg — the heat needed to convert 1 kg of ice at 0 °C into water at 0 °C.

Latent heat of vaporisation of water = 22.5 × 10⁵ J/kg — the heat needed to convert 1 kg of water at 100 °C into steam at 100 °C.

In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the margin → definition first, with “1 kg” and “without change in temperature” underlined → the two values as two separate lines, each with its unit J/kg boxed. No diagram for this question.
How the marks are given
  • 1 mark — definition carrying both key phrases: “1 kg” and “without change in temperature.”
  • 1 mark each — each value with its unit, J/kg. A value without a unit is an incomplete answer.
Common mistake: swapping the two values, or writing them per gram. Vaporisation (22.5 × 10⁵) is far bigger than fusion (3.34 × 10⁵) — breaking particles completely free takes much more energy than just loosening them. Remember it by logic, not by rote.

Q6. Why does steam cause more severe burns than boiling water, though both are at 100 °C?

Steam contains extra energy in the form of the latent heat of vaporisation (22.5 × 10⁵ J/kg), which boiling water does not have. When steam touches the skin, it first condenses and releases this extra heat, and then the heat of the hot water formed acts on the skin as well. So steam delivers more heat to the skin than the same amount of boiling water, and the burns are more severe.

In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the margin → line 1: steam carries latent heat → line 2: it condenses on skin and releases it → line 3: conclusion. Two marks, three lines — no diagram, no introduction.
How the marks are given
  • 1 mark — steam carries latent heat of vaporisation in addition to its heat at 100 °C.
  • 1 mark — on contact it condenses, releasing that extra heat onto the skin.
Common mistake: “Steam is hotter than boiling water.” At the same pressure both are at 100 °C — the difference is not temperature, it is stored latent heat. Writing “hotter” loses the first mark immediately.

Q7. Ice at 273 K is more effective in cooling a drink than water at 273 K. Why?

Both are at the same temperature, but ice can absorb extra heat from the drink as the latent heat of fusion (3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg) to convert itself into water at 273 K. Water at 273 K can only take heat by warming up. So ice removes more heat from the drink and cools it more effectively.

In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the margin → line 1: ice absorbs latent heat of fusion from the drink → line 2: water at 273 K cannot → conclusion in the same line. No diagram.
How the marks are given
  • 1 mark — ice absorbs latent heat of fusion from the surroundings (the drink) in order to melt.
  • 1 mark — water at the same temperature has no such extra heat absorption, so it cools less.
Common mistake: “Ice is colder than water.” Both are at 273 K — identical temperature. The examiner is testing whether you reach for latent heat instead of the word “colder.”

Q8. What is evaporation? State and explain the factors which affect the rate of evaporation.

Evaporation is the change of a liquid into its vapour from its surface, taking place at all temperatures. Its rate depends on four factors:

  1. Surface area — evaporation is a surface phenomenon, so a larger exposed surface lets more particles escape. Water dries faster in a plate than in a bottle.
  2. Temperature — at higher temperature, more particles have enough kinetic energy to escape, so evaporation increases. Clothes dry faster in the sun.
  3. Humidity — if the air already contains a large amount of water vapour, it can hold less more, so the rate of evaporation decreases. Clothes dry slowly on a humid day.
  4. Wind speed — wind carries the vapour particles away from the surface, so evaporation increases. Clothes dry faster on a windy day.
In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the margin → definition first (surface + all temperatures underlined) → four numbered factors, name of factor in bold at the start of each line, direction of effect, one example each → stop at factor 4. No diagram earns marks here.
How the marks are given
  • 1 mark — definition with both key phrases: “from the surface” and “at all temperatures.”
  • 1 mark per factor — the factor named and the direction of its effect explained; an everyday example is the safest way to show the direction.
Common mistake: listing the four factors with no explanation — that earns half marks at best. And for humidity, the direction is the trap: more humidity → slower evaporation, not faster.

Q10. Why does the water kept in an earthen pot (matka) become cool in summer?

An earthen pot is porous. Water from inside keeps seeping out through the pores to the outer surface, where it evaporates. For evaporation, the water takes the required latent heat from the water stored inside the pot. The stored water therefore loses heat, its temperature falls, and it becomes cool.

In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the margin → mechanism as a 3-step chain: pores → evaporation at the outer surface → latent heat taken from the stored water. Draw the matka diagram HERE — after the written chain, centre of the page, in pencil, with exactly 3 labels: pores, water seeps out, evaporation cools the water inside.
How the marks are given
  • 1 mark — porous pot: water seeps through pores and evaporates from the outer surface.
  • 1 mark — the evaporating water takes its latent heat from the stored water, so the stored water cools.
Common mistake: “Earthen pots are bad conductors, so the water stays cool.” Conductivity is not the mechanism — a steel pot with the same water does not cool it. If your answer never says the word evaporation, it scores zero here.

Q11. (a) Convert 293 K and 470 K to the Celsius scale. (b) Convert 25 °C and 373 °C to the Kelvin scale.

The relation between the scales is:

Temperature in K = Temperature in °C + 273  ·  °C = K − 273

(a) 293 K = 293 − 273 = 20 °C  ·  470 K = 470 − 273 = 197 °C

(b) 25 °C = 25 + 273 = 298 K  ·  373 °C = 373 + 273 = 646 K

In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the margin → write the formula line first → each conversion on its own line with substitution shown → answers in a box, units written, no degree symbol on kelvin.
How the marks are given
  • 1 mark — the relation written once, correctly, before any numbers.
  • ½ mark per conversion — substitution shown, correct answer, correct unit. Kelvin answers carry no degree symbol: write 298 K, never 298 °K.
Common mistake: adding 273 when converting from kelvin. Fix it with one anchor you can never forget: kelvin numbers are always the bigger ones (0 °C = 273 K). If your kelvin answer came out smaller than the Celsius one, you went the wrong way.

Write & check — three written questions with value points

3 marks · “Give reason” set

Give reasons: (a) A gas fills completely the vessel in which it is kept. (b) A gas exerts pressure on the walls of its container. (c) The smell of perfume reaches us even when we are sitting several metres away.

  • (a) Gas particles move rapidly in all directions and interparticle attraction is negligible, so they spread to fill the whole available space — 1 mark.
  • (b) Particles move randomly at high speed and collide with the container walls; these collisions exert pressure — 1 mark.
  • (c) Perfume particles are continuously moving and diffuse into air, spreading in all directions until they reach us — 1 mark. Key word the examiner looks for: diffusion.
2 marks · Numerical

(a) Convert 573 K into Celsius. (b) Convert 35 °C into Kelvin. (c) Which is hotter — 300 K or 45 °C?

  • (a) 573 − 273 = 300 °C — ½ mark for the formula/substitution, ½ for the answer with unit.
  • (b) 35 + 273 = 308 K.
  • (c) Convert first: 45 °C = 318 K, which is more than 300 K — so 45 °C is hotter. The conversion step itself earns the mark; a bare guess earns nothing.
3 marks · HOTS

Why does our palm feel cold when we put a few drops of acetone (nail-polish remover) on it, colder than it feels with water?

  • Acetone evaporates much faster than water — 1 mark.
  • To evaporate, its particles take the needed energy (latent heat) from the palm — 1 mark.
  • The palm loses heat and its temperature falls, so it feels cold — 1 mark. Chain your answer in exactly this order: evaporation → energy taken from palm → temperature falls.

A model 5-mark answer — written the way it should look in the copy

Read it twice: once for the science, once for the shape. The notes on the right are what the examiner is ticking.

Q. “We are advised to wear cotton clothes in summer.” Justify this statement by explaining the cooling caused by evaporation. Give two more examples of evaporation causing cooling in daily life. (5 marks)
Evaporation and cooling: Evaporation is the change of a liquid into vapour from its surface at all temperatures. The particles at the surface that escape are those with the highest kinetic energy, so the average kinetic energy of the particles left behind decreases. As a result, the temperature of the liquid falls — evaporation causes cooling.
1 markDefinition + the mechanism. Both halves needed: naming the process alone earns nothing here.
Why cotton in summer: In summer our body produces more sweat. Cotton is a good absorber of water — it soaks up the sweat and exposes it to the atmosphere over a large surface area. The sweat then evaporates easily, taking the required latent heat from our body, and we feel cool and comfortable.
2 marksApplies the mechanism to the exact situation asked: absorb → expose → evaporate → body loses heat. One mark for the chain, one for linking it back to the statement.
Two more examples: (1) Water kept in an earthen pot (matka) stays cool, because water seeps through its pores and evaporates from the outer surface. (2) A desert cooler cools a room on a hot, dry day, because the water in its pads evaporates continuously and takes heat from the air passing through.
2 marksOne mark per example — and each example still names the mechanism instead of just naming the object.

Notice what is not in that answer: no introduction paragraph, no conclusion, no repeated definition. Five marks, five tickable points, key terms underlined, done. Length was never the goal — visible value points are.

Q. What is evaporation? Explain in detail the factors affecting the rate of evaporation.

Evaporation is the phenomenon of change of a liquid into vapours at any temperature below its boiling point, taking place from the surface of the liquid. The rate of evaporation depends on four factors:

  1. Surface area — evaporation is a surface phenomenon; the larger the surface exposed, the faster the evaporation. Water dries faster in a plate than in a bottle.
  2. Temperature — at higher temperature, more particles have enough kinetic energy to escape into the vapour state, so evaporation increases.
  3. Humidity — humidity is the amount of water vapour already present in air. If humidity is high, the air accepts vapour slowly and the rate of evaporation decreases. Clothes dry slowly in the monsoon.
  4. Wind speed — wind carries vapour particles away from above the surface, so evaporation increases. Clothes dry faster on a windy day.
In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the margin → definition as the opening paragraph → four numbered factors, each starting with the factor name in bold, direction next, one everyday example in the same line or the next → finish with the humidity line underlined, it is the trap factor → no diagram and no separate conclusion for this question.
How the marks are given
  • 1 mark — definition with “surface” and “below the boiling point / at any temperature”.
  • 1 mark per factor — factor named, direction of effect stated, example or mechanism attached.
Common mistake: Listing the four factor names with no direction or explanation — half marks at best. And writing that humidity speeds evaporation up.
3 marksApplication of evaporationCBSE 2026 pattern · also NCERT p. 10 Q5

Q. What type of clothes should we wear in summer, and why?

We should wear cotton clothes in summer. During summer we perspire more. Cotton is a good absorber of water — it absorbs the sweat and exposes it to the atmosphere over a large surface area. The sweat evaporates easily, taking the heat equal to the latent heat of vaporisation from the body, so the body feels cool and comfortable.

In your answer copy — layoutQ.No. in the margin → answer (“cotton”) in the first line → the mechanism as one flowing paragraph in the chain order above → stop.
How the marks are given
  • 1 mark — cotton named.
  • 2 marks — the chain: absorbs sweat → exposes it to air → evaporation → latent heat taken from the body → cooling.
Common mistake: Stopping at “cotton absorbs sweat” — absorption alone earns only the first mark; the evaporation chain earns the rest.

Mistakes that quietly cost marks in this chapter

  • “Liquids have a fixed shape.” They don't — fixed volume, shape of the container. This exact confusion is a favourite MCQ and case-study trap.
  • Treating evaporation and boiling as the same thing. Surface vs bulk, all temperatures vs one fixed temperature — if a question says “at all temperatures”, it is evaporation.
  • Forgetting to convert before comparing temperatures, and dropping units (J/kg, K) from latent-heat and conversion answers.
  • Calling sugar “not a solid” because the heap takes the jar's shape. Judge the single crystal, not the heap.
  • Writing that temperature rises during melting. During a change of state, supplied heat becomes latent heat — the thermometer does not move.
  • Assertion–reason: picking “R is the correct explanation” just because both statements are true. Test the “because” link.
